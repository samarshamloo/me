Telecom / Irancell

Redesigning a loan journey where only 0.42% of users received a loan

Role: Product Designer (flow, structure & UI) Scope: UX & UI Team: Atefeh Safari (Product Manager), Arash Asghari (Visual Designer), Bahar Hajavi (Product Designer) Status: In development

Context

Irancell's digital lending product lets users get a loan and spend it on purchases, including on Irancell Marketplace, which makes it valuable for both users and the business. But the product wasn't attracting its target customers: the earlier design left the journey complex, with high drop-off at several stages. The goal was to cut drop-off and raise conversion, quickly, within Irancell's design system.

The problem

56,129 users entered. 238 received a loan: 0.42%. The drop-off was spread across three places, and not all of it was ours to fix:

  • Before choosing a loan: ~23,738 users (~42%). Of ~24,500 users at step zero, only ~500 had arrived in the last three days, so we counted the rest as dropped.

  • Credit validation: 13,599 rejected, 6,039 stuck waiting, 5,780 cancelled. Some of this is genuine credit rejection, outside design's reach. But some isn't: users who never came back for the result, or who just wanted a free credit check.

  • Guarantee cheque: 3,934 users entered, only 47 reached contract signing.

This data was provided to us by Irancell's product team.

Approach: learn from the best, fix what matters most

I studied the loan journeys of leading lending services, such as Snapp Pay, DigiPay and TechnoPay, to bring Irancell's flow closer to patterns users already know. Three things stood out:

  • Users never have to guess. The process is explained clearly at every step.

  • Automation and information at the point of need, like OTP and collecting details only when required. Even asking for a birth date was simpler.

  • Transparent loan plans: installment, total repayment and the subscription fee shown upfront. In Irancell's flow, users only learned about the subscription at the very last step.

Then I fixed high-impact problems before polishing the UI. In phase one I started with what hurt conversion most and took the least effort to change: the places where the experience was broken and users were being lost. I designed both the structure and the UI of the core screens myself. Visual refinement came in stages afterward, and competitor extras were left until we reached a solid minimal experience.

What I did

1. Before choosing a loan: ~23,738 users

Why users dropped: The old flow asked for national ID, birth date, postal code and landline before users had seen a single plan. The loan cards showed only a bank name, a loan type and an amount, and anything more meant opening a details page. Users were asked to give information with nothing in return, and couldn't compare anything.

What I did:

  • Loans first, personal information later. Users see their plans before being asked for anything, and I collect details only at the point they're needed.
  • Plan cards that say everything: bank, profit, fee, subscription fee and total repayment on the card, with chips for subscription and guarantee type that open the details. Users learn upfront that the loan needs a subscription, instead of discovering it at the last step.
  • A home screen that reads as a starting point. I replaced the wallet balance of "0", which feels like a loss, with a prompt to choose a plan, and added a notifications section.
  • An easier way in: imagery that explains the loan's benefits, automatic OTP, and a rebuilt birth-date field.
2. Credit validation: 13,599 rejected, 6,039 waiting, 5,780 cancelled

Why users dropped: The old screens showed a pointless credit-check fee, gave a rating with little explanation, and said "we can't serve you now" on a rejection. The "up to 24 hours" wait was usually far longer than the check took, and when the credit-scoring system was down, users were never told.

What I did:

  • Explained what's being checked: repayment history, returned-cheque status and bank transaction balance.
  • Made every outcome clear and actionable:
    • Qualified: shows the rating and the chosen plan, and users continue.
    • Rating too low: says a higher rating is needed.
    • Mid rating: offers a smaller loan as an alternative plan, a hook so the user isn't lost.
  • Communicated system outages instead of hiding them behind a vague wait.
  • Removed the "0 Rial" fee.
3. Guarantee cheque: 3,934 users, 47 reached signing

Why users dropped: One very long page, a pile of fields, and no guidance.

What I did: I redesigned the cheque experience around clarity, and Bahar later restructured it into three steps (cheque image, Sayad registration, physical delivery) and refined it further.

My part:

  • The physical cheque requirements, presented visually and cleanly, so users could read them in full
  • Easier fields for entering cheque information
  • Copy buttons beside the details users must carry into the Sayad system, and the postal details for express delivery
  • Clear reasons when a cheque is accepted or rejected, so users know what to fix
Across the whole journey
  • One clear task per step, the principle I designed around.
  • Errors users can recover from: a human message that explains the cause and what to do, with the typed data preserved.
  • A confirmation checkbox where users must have certain items to continue, so anyone who doesn't qualify stops early instead of wasting time and costing Irancell money.
  • A profile (my information, my requests, transactions, guide, FAQ, rules, logout) and push notifications, because SMS alone is unreliable. Logout used to throw users out of the flow.
Outcome

The result is a loan journey built around clarity, less friction and user confidence.

Before After
Information requested before users could even see a loan Loans first, personal information only at the point it's needed
Subscription fee discovered at the last step Fee shown upfront, on the plan itself
"You don't qualify," with no reason Predictable statuses and explained rejection reasons
One very long, error-prone cheque page A guarantee cheque process with one clear task per step

AI Assist / ByGiraffe

An AI stylist, built from zero

Role: Product Designer Platform: Mobile App Timeline: 4 Months Scope: Product Discovery, UX/UI Design, Wireframing, Design System Deliverables: 111+ Screens

Context

BYGiraffe is an AI-powered fashion assistant designed to help users discover their personal style, explore fashion items, and find pieces that work well together.

The product brings style discovery, personalized shopping recommendations, and AI-assisted styling into one mobile experience.

The Challenge

Finding clothes that match personal preferences and putting together a complete look can be overwhelming. Users may know what they like, but still need guidance when choosing complementary items or exploring new styles.

The goal was to create an experience that helps users understand their style preferences and use them throughout their shopping journey.

Product Discovery & Competitive Analysis

I reviewed and refined the initial PRD, helping clarify product features and user journeys. I also conducted competitor research to understand existing solutions, identify common patterns, and explore opportunities for BYGiraffe.

The analysis covered six products — Acloset, Style DNA, Whering, Gensmo, Aiuta, and Pinterest — across ten key dimensions, including personal style recommendations, image recognition, digital closet management, content creation, and AI integration.

One opportunity identified in our comparison was the potential to bring style discovery, product recommendations, and outfit completion together in a more connected shopping experience.

Approach

The experience was structured around three key stages:

  1. Discover Your Style — Understand users’ preferences through visual onboarding.
  2. Explore & Personalize — Help users discover relevant products and complementary items.
  3. Style with Confidence — Offer outfit previews and accessible AI styling assistance.

What I Did

1. Product Structure & Wireframing

I mapped the core user journeys and translated the product requirements into structured flows and wireframes. This helped define the main interactions and establish a consistent foundation before moving into visual design.

2. Style Discovery & Personal Profile

I designed a visual onboarding experience where users could select images that reflect their fashion preferences. These selections helped shape a personal style profile and informed the recommendations shown throughout the app.

The experience also included style inspiration through suggested celebrity looks.

3. Personalized Shopping & Complete Your Style

I designed a personalized product discovery experience based on users’ style preferences.

The Complete Your Style feature helped users find complementary items around a selected product. For example, after choosing a T-shirt, users could explore matching trousers, shoes, and bags to complete the look.

4. Avatar-Based Outfit Preview

I designed an outfit preview experience using two predefined avatars — one male and one female. Users could preview selected clothing items on the corresponding avatar and explore how different pieces work together.

5. AI Styling Assistant

I designed AI Giraffe as a floating entry point that remained accessible across different app screens. This allowed users to open the assistant from various parts of the experience and ask questions related to fashion and styling.

6. Visual Language & Design System

I developed a minimal visual language and a reusable design system to maintain consistency across the product. Components and interface patterns were designed to support a cohesive experience across more than 110 screens.

Outcome

Nonprofit / Mahak

From a donation website to an engaging digital experience

Role: Product Designer Duration:9 months Focus: UX, UI, Information Architecture, UX Writing Team: Atefeh Safari (Product Manager) Status: Live

Context

Mahak is a charity whose app was built mainly around financial giving and direct donations. The goal was to make the experience more appealing and understandable for younger users, and to give people more ways to learn about Mahak's work and support it. The app has ~240K installs.

The problem

The app did one thing, accept donations, and several things held people back:

  • The navigation and product sections weren't self-explanatory.
  • Users could create campaigns but often didn't know how to reach the campaign's goal.
  • The long campaign form made the process hard.
  • Users couldn't easily see where their donations went or what impact they had.
  • Product language, messages and states needed to be clearer and more human.
  • A static, repetitive homepage gave little reason to come back.

Approach

I restructured the experience around the actions users needed most, instead of exposing every feature equally, and widened what "supporting Mahak" could mean: donate, campaign, stories, Empathy Cards, B2B partnerships.

What I did

1. Navigation: a clearer structure and visible impact

Why users struggled: The main menu didn't make the sections or their priority clear, and users couldn't tell where their money went.

What I did:

  • I rethought the main menu. The bottom navigation went from Home, Projects, Support, Campaign, Account to Home, Cash Support, Me & Mahak. I moved campaigns out of the bottom menu, since visibility alone wasn't helping users understand them.
  • I renamed "Projects" to "Where your donations go", so users see where their money is spent and trust grows.
  • Cash support already had a clear visual presence, so the change was in priority and structure, not in introducing it again.

2. Campaign creation: from a long form to a finishable flow

Why users struggled: A long, one-page form, and users didn't know how to take a campaign to its goal.

What I did:

  • I turned the form into three guided steps, with a progress indicator, and pre-selected an urgent-support project so starting was easier, while keeping the option to change it.
  • I clarified what a campaign is and how to introduce it, working with UX writing.
  • I designed feedback for rejected campaigns, so users learn why, respectfully: understandable reasons (such as an unsuitable goal, misleading content or a policy conflict), the notification text, the path to the admin's feedback, an optional explanation, and the decision's status and history.

3. A homepage that feels alive

Why users struggled: The old homepage was long, repetitive and hard to scan.

What I did: I redesigned it from static to dynamic: clear, purpose-driven sections, less repetitive and low-priority content, dynamic content and card-based previews, a clearer hierarchy and CTAs, and a more scannable layout. It now connects what Mahak does, how users take part, and how they can give.

4. Communication that sounds human

Why users struggled: The tone was formal and distant, and messages and states were unclear.

What I did:

  • I rewrote and designed the UX writing for key flows, notifications, popups, error messages and empty states.
  • I designed the logic for when to ask for notification permission, and it's largely live: notifications are now sent following these patterns. The system request appears after a meaningful interaction rather than at first launch, within the first 24 to 72 hours, with a cap on how often it's shown, re-asked only at the right moments, and with events defined to measure its performance.

5. Digital greetings: a familiar gesture, made easier

Mahak's website already offered condolence and congratulation cards, in the spirit of its physical stands. I reimagined the idea for the app: easier to use, more modern, and flexible enough for many different occasions.

I designed the flow: choose a card → personalize sender and recipient → send → pay → optional donation. It links an everyday emotional need to a chance to support Mahak, and creates a new donation touchpoint.

6. More ways to stay connected

I designed support reminders and return flows, sharing the app, referrals, a notifications section, profile improvements, engagement communications, and the visual design for B2B partnerships.

Outcome

The biggest shift: from simply asking users to donate, to giving them more ways to understand, engage with and support Mahak.
